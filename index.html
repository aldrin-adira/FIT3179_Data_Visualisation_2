01

A worldwide bond

Australia in a selected international comparison.

A shared bond, different surveys

These surveys cover 43 countries, mostly in Europe. Australia has one of the highest reported shares of homes with a dog. Each country is shaded by its reported percentage of households owning a dog.

The map compares the same measure in every country. The dot rows below compare dogs and cats separately; their percentages cannot be added because some homes have both. Survey years and methods differ. Malaysia is not part of this selection, so it appears grey.

A strong position in this selection

Australia ranks fourth for dog ownership in this selection, and eighth for cats. These are selected-country rankings, not world rankings.

From international context to the homes behind Australia’s growth.

02

More homes, more companions

Growth in ownership meets the realities of housing.

More households; a higher share

Pet ownership rose from 62% in 2016 to 73% in 2025. Pet-owning households grew from 5.7 million to 7.7 million. Both the share of homes and the number of homes matter.

+11more homes in every 100 have a pet: from 62 to 73.

Housing shapes what is possible

Families with children at home and mortgage holders each report 85% ownership. Apartment households report 57%: the closest comparison for Malaysians living in flats and condominiums. These are differences between groups; they do not prove that housing causes ownership.

73%The national benchmark helps put each group in context.

Different homes also make room for different kinds of pet.

03

A companion that fits

The most common pet is not the most numerous.

One dog, or a tank of fish

Dogs reach the most homes, yet fish have led the animal count in every survey since 2016: about 12.1 million fish compared with 7.4 million dogs in 2025. Below them, birds briefly passed dogs in 2019, then slipped to fourth as cats moved up. Follow a line to trace one pet's rank.

Four national surveys, 2016 to 2025. Equal rank gaps do not mean equal differences in quantity.

Different expectations

Companionship is the leading published reason for dogs, cats, birds and small mammals. Fish owners favour relaxation; reptile owners favour lower maintenance. Read down each pet's column to compare its published reasons.

Hatching means outside the published top five, not zero. Owners could give several reasons.

A suitable companion also needs affordable, ongoing care.

04

The price of companionship

Day-to-day care is part of the decision.

Food takes the largest share

A dog-owning home spends about 2,520 Australian dollars a year on its dogs (about 6,960 ringgit); a cat-owning home about 1,656 Australian dollars (about 4,570 ringgit). Food is the largest single cost, 1,090 Australian dollars for dogs and 884 Australian dollars for cats, but health care comes next: 745 Australian dollars for dogs and 490 Australian dollars for cats once veterinary visits, medicines, parasite treatments, supplements and insurance are added together.

Follow each pet through broad cost groups to the reported categories. Branch width shows dollars on one scale; labels retain even the smallest costs. The dog categories sum to 2,521 Australian dollars, one Australian dollar above the published total because of rounding. Ringgit figures convert Australian prices at the 2025 average rate of 2.76 ringgit per Australian dollar (Bank Negara Malaysia); they are not Malaysian prices.

Wanting a pet is only the start

Housing restrictions, cost and responsibility can stand between wanting a pet and getting one. Compare reasons within each side: the two sides ask different groups of people.

These are reasons people gave, not measured causes. Malaysia has a similar housing question: strata by-laws let condominium residents keep pets unless an animal causes nuisance or danger, though management can add rules by special resolution (The Star, 2026).

Local conditions add another layer to the national picture.

05

One country, different local lives

South Australia shows why totals and rates tell different stories.

Large populations can produce large pet totals. A rate divides by residents to make council comparisons fairer. The left map sizes one circle by each council’s dog total; the right map shows how common recorded animals are for the number of people. Both maps show dogs over the same area, so places line up.

68 municipal councils · Animal records: 30 May 2025 · Population: 30 June 2025. Recorded animals are not a household ownership estimate. The maps show the settled south. Coober Pedy and Roxby Downs lie outside this view; the scatterplot includes all 68 councils.

Municipal council records only. Blank areas lie outside the council dataset. The rate colours show dogs per 1,000 residents in steps of 100.

Settlement patterns matter

Councils where people live closer together tend to record fewer dogs per resident. Cats show a weaker pattern.

Each step along the bottom axis is ten times more crowded. The lines show the overall trend; they do not prove that crowding causes fewer dogs.

Behind these differences is a shared motivation: companionship.

06

A pet-loving nation

The reward is emotional. The commitment is practical.

Why the bond endures

About half of dog and cat owners name companionship; about one in four name mental health or relaxation. These answers describe expectations; they are not evidence that owning a pet improves health.

Whether in Sydney or Kuala Lumpur, lasting companionship takes a suitable home, affordable care and time. Matching those commitments to the household helps the bond last.

Notes

The local dataset includes all animals marked “At Home”, across registration statuses. Conflicting registration-year notes prevent a registered-only interpretation. Interstate and Outback Communities Authority records are excluded from the 68 municipal councils. The animal snapshot and population reference dates differ by one month.

Sections 1 to 4 and 6 use estimates from household surveys, not counts. In Section 1, each country's figure comes from its own national survey, with different methods and years. For its full sample, the Animal Medicines Australia report gives a maximum error margin of plus or minus 2.0 percentage points at the 95% confidence level; the margin is larger for smaller groups, such as small mammal and reptile owners. Percentages may not add to 100 because households can keep several types of pet, owners could give several reasons, and values are rounded. Every number can be checked against the project data files. Section 5 uses council records instead, which count only the animals owners have recorded, so differences between councils partly reflect how fully owners comply. Ringgit amounts are currency conversions of Australian prices, not estimates of what pet care costs in Malaysia.
